Tomorrow's weather is a guess. Every load forecast is graded as if it weren't.

Models that decide how much power a grid commits are backtested against the temperature that actually occurred — an answer key they will never have on the day. This is a measurement of what that difference costs.

The landscape demand against temperature

This terrain is the finding.

Texas demand is flat between about 15 and 20 °C and climbs steeply at both ends — air conditioning above, electric heating below. Plotted, it is a valley with mountains on either side.

You are standing on the summer peak. The descent ahead is the comfort zone. Beyond it the ground climbs again, into the freeze.

Confidence conformal prediction intervals

It overstates confidence, not just accuracy.

A point forecast carries none of this, so the next step is a range — calibrated on the model's own recent residuals, per temperature band.

Calibrate on observed-weather residuals, the natural thing to do, and a nominal 90% interval covers 83.8% in practice. Calibrating honestly recovers most of it, for about 8% more width.

That is the more dangerous error. A too-narrow interval is exactly what persuades someone to skip the review.

The decision where does the review line go?

The model gives a number. Someone still sets the line.

If some hours cannot be trusted, route them to a person. Reviewing costs analyst time; errors cost money. How many hours you review is not a statistical question. It is a ratio.

What does not work and why that is the finding

Some hours cannot be flagged at all.

Four decision-time uncertainty signals were combined to rank hours for review. Reviewing the worst 5% moves error from 2.92% to 2.77%. Picking at random gets 2.94%.

The entire benefit is 0.17 percentage points — and the single worst hour, 14,164 MW, is never flagged at any budget up to 10%.

Why the ceiling exists

The worst hours are 10–14 GW out, almost all on 26–27 January 2026. Two rounds of feature engineering aimed at them moved the error almost not at all.

The reason is in the operator's own numbers. ERCOT missed the same hours, in the same direction, by up to 9,616 MW. That morning demand fell while the temperature held near −6 °C.

Demand dropping during a freeze is not a weather response. It is behaviour — a conservation appeal, or large load being curtailed.

You cannot build a confidence score for an event whose cause is not among your inputs.

Every signal here is a function of temperature. The dominant error source is not. Catching those hours needs a different input — a demand-response feed, the operator's own conservation notices — not better statistics.

Which is worth knowing before you buy a better model.

Every lit window is a forecast that was close enough.

Not the most accurate one. The one whose error someone had measured honestly, and priced, before the weather arrived.
